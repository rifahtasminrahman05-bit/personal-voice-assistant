Research is a disciplined process of asking a question, gathering evidence, and reaching a justified answer.
The scientific method: observe, ask a question, form a hypothesis, test it with an experiment, analyze the results, and revise or conclude.
A good research question is specific, answerable with evidence, and worth asking. Narrow beats broad: how does sleep affect memory recall in students beats what affects memory.
Primary sources report original data or first hand accounts. Secondary sources interpret primary sources. Tertiary sources like encyclopedias summarize; they are good starting points, not final evidence.
Source evaluation: who wrote it, what are their credentials, when was it published, is it peer reviewed, who funded it, and can the claims be verified elsewhere.
Peer review means independent experts check a paper's methods and reasoning before publication. It filters errors but is not proof of truth; replication matters more.
Citations give credit and let readers verify claims. Every factual statement in a report should trace to a source. Quote accurately and note the page.
Bias comes in forms: selection bias when samples are not representative, confirmation bias when we seek evidence that favors our belief, and publication bias when only positive results get printed.
Correlation does not imply causation. Ice cream sales and drownings rise together because of summer, not because ice cream is dangerous. Controlled experiments and randomization separate causes from coincidence.
Sample size matters. Small samples produce noisy results. A p value shows how surprising the data would be if there were no real effect; it does not measure importance.
Taking notes while researching: capture the idea in your own words, record the source and page, and mark direct quotes clearly. This prevents accidental plagiarism later.
Synthesis combines findings from many sources into one coherent picture. Compare sources: where they agree, evidence is strong; where they conflict, dig into methods and dates.
Search skills: use specific phrases, quotes for exact matches, site filters for trusted domains, and multiple search engines. Check the second page, not just the first.
Reading a paper efficiently: abstract first, then conclusion, then figures, then methods. Decide quickly whether the full read is worth it.
Literature review maps what is already known, where sources disagree, and what gap remains. The gap justifies new work.
Data cleaning is most of data work: handle missing values, remove duplicates, check units, and document every transformation so others can repeat it.
Reproducibility means another person can rerun your analysis and get the same result. Share code, data, versions, and environment.
A literature note has three parts: the claim, the evidence behind it, and your own assessment. Keep claims and opinions separate.
Fact checking steps: find the original source, check the date, look for the same claim in independent sources, and watch for out of context quotes.
Research ethics: informed consent, privacy protection, honest reporting of results including negative ones, and no fabrication or selective omission.
Writing up: state the question, the method, the evidence, the limitations, and the conclusion. Limitations are not weaknesses to hide; they show careful thinking.
Summarizing a document: find the thesis, list the main supporting points, and compress each into one sentence in your own words.
Open questions drive progress. Good researchers write down what they do not know and design the next step to test it.
Cross checking with multiple independent sources is the strongest defense against being misled by a single wrong source.
